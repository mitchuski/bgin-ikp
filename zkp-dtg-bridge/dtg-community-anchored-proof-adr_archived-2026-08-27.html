Community-Anchored Proof

Choosing the first zero-knowledge use case to implement against DTG credentials — and what any implementation of it must satisfy.

Record
ADR-001
Status
Proposed

Audience

Decision

The first zero-knowledge proof we implement against DTG credentials will be the Community-Anchored Proof already described in the DTG Core Credentials specification: proving that a relationship exists inside a shared community, without revealing who is in it.

This record defines what must be proven and what any implementation must satisfy. It deliberately does not choose how to build it.

01Why we need to pick one

Zero-knowledge proofs are easy to talk about in the abstract and hard to evaluate. A demonstration that proves someone is over eighteen is impressive, but it tells us nothing about whether a trust graph survives being made private — any credential system can do it.

We need a first use case that is small enough to build, and specific enough that succeeding at it proves something real. It should be one the specification already describes, so implementing it is conformance rather than invention, and one that cannot be quietly satisfied by simpler means such as disclosing fewer fields of a credential.

The Community-Anchored Proof meets all three conditions.

02The use case

Nia asks a maintainer for access to a restricted project. The maintainer's rule is simple:

Someone already inside our community must have a working relationship with you.

Nia can satisfy it. Nia holds a relationship credential issued by Omar, who Nia has worked with, and a membership credential from community C. Omar holds a membership credential from community C too.

Today, the only way for Nia to demonstrate this is to hand over the credentials themselves. They name the private identifiers Nia and Omar use with each other and nobody else. So the maintainer learns not just that the rule is met, but exactly who vouched for Nia — and gains an identifier that will recognise Nia on every future request, and that other verifiers holding the same credential can compare notes on.

Nia is answering a yes-or-no question and paying for it with a permanent, correlatable disclosure of a private relationship.

What the maintainer learns today

  • Which community member vouched for Nia
  • The private identifiers naming that relationship
  • A handle that recognises Nia on every later request
  • Something other verifiers can correlate against

What the maintainer needs to learn

  • The rule is met
  • Nothing else

The statement Nia should be able to make is: “A member of community C has a working relationship with me, and I am also a member of community C.” The maintainer checks it, gets a yes, and learns nothing further — not who Nia is, not who vouched, and not enough to recognise Nia the next time.

This generalises well beyond project access. It is the shape of every introduction, referral, reference check and vouching flow in a trust graph: the verifier needs the edge to be real and anchored in a community they recognise, and has no legitimate need for the identities at either end of it.

03What must be proven

Three things, together, in a single proof:

  1. The holder possesses a valid relationship credential.
  2. The holder possesses a valid membership credential from community C.
  3. The party who issued that relationship credential also holds a valid membership credential from the same community C.The hard one

Clauses 1 and 2 are statements about credentials the holder has. Clause 3 is a statement about someone else's credential — one the holder cannot sign, cannot forge, and must prove without the other party being online, asked, or even aware.

That is what makes this a genuine test. There is no way to satisfy clause 3 by revealing less of what you hold; either an implementation can make a sound statement about a third party's membership or it cannot. It is a clean pass or fail, which is exactly what a first implementation should give us.

04Requirements

These are the properties an implementation must satisfy, stated so they can be tested. How they are achieved is out of scope — see section 05.

Privacy

P1The proof reveals no relationship identifier from the relationship credential.
P2The proof reveals no identifier of the counterparty who issued it.
P3The proof reveals no identifier of the holder, beyond attributes the holder deliberately chooses to disclose.
P4Two proofs built from the same credentials cannot be linked to one another — not by a single verifier, and not by verifiers who compare what they received.
P5A verifier learns the outcome and any deliberately disclosed attributes, and nothing further.

Soundness

S1A party lacking the required credentials cannot produce a proof that verifies.
S2Clause 3 cannot be satisfied unless the counterparty genuinely holds a membership credential from the same community.
S3The counterparty need not be online, consulted, or aware when the proof is made.
S4The presenter proves they are the subject of the credentials, not merely a party holding a copy of them.
S5A proof is bound to a challenge from the verifier and cannot be replayed to another verifier or at another time.

Currency

C1A proof does not verify if any credential it relies on has been revoked or suspended.
C2A proof states the registry state it was made against, and a verifier can judge whether that state is recent enough for its purposes.
C3Establishing that a credential is current must not itself identify the holder.
C4The delay between a change — a departure, a revocation — and proofs reflecting it is bounded and published.

Registry

T1A verifier can confirm the community is one it recognises, without the holder revealing which member of it they are.
T2Whatever a proof relies on from a trust registry is independently checkable by a party that did not create it.
T3Two verifiers checking the same proof against the same registry state reach the same verdict.
T4A registry's obligations are stated as an interface, so that any conformant registry can serve these proofs.

Deployment

D1Producing a proof is feasible on the devices that actually hold credentials — a phone, or an agent acting for a person — within a stated time and memory budget.
D2Verifying a proof is cheap enough to run inline while handling a request.
D3A proof fits the transports DTG credentials already travel over.

Conformance and governance

X1Published test vectors covering both proofs that must verify and proofs that must fail.
X2An implementation built independently from the specification can verify another implementation's proofs.
X3Any requirement this places on how credentials are issued is stated explicitly and early, because it changes what issuers must do.
G1A community can declare in its governance whether private presentation is required, default, or optional.
G2The assurance a proof carries is traceable to the governance of the community that issued the membership credentials.

05Explicitly out of scope

This record chooses a use case and sets the bar. It does not choose an implementation, and no part of it should be read as favouring one.

  • The proof system. The specification is format-agnostic and this record keeps it that way.
  • The construction. How the three clauses are expressed and combined into one proof.
  • How a registry publishes what proofs rely on. The requirements say it must be independently checkable; they do not say by what means.
  • Encodings and wire formats for proofs and challenges.
  • Identity proofing itself. How a community decides someone is a person, or unique, is governance and is unchanged by this work.
  • Delegation chains. Proving an agent may act for a member of a recognised community is the natural next case, and deserves its own record once this one is proven.

06Which working groups this touches

This use case sits deliberately across four groups, because it is the seam between them that has never been tested.

Working groupWhat this record asks of them
DTG Credentials Confirm the three clauses match the Community-Anchored Proof as specified, and identify anything it requires of credential issuance.
ZKP Choose the construction, build a reference prover and verifier, and publish the test vectors called for by X1 and X2.
Trust Registry Define what a registry must expose for a proof to rely on it, and how currency and revocation are expressed. The specification currently leaves this to future work; the requirements in sections C and T are that work.
Governance Define how a community declares whether private presentation is required, default, or optional, and how the assurance of a proof traces back to governance.

The registry seam is worth calling out. Clause 3 asks a holder to prove something about another member's standing, which means a registry has to expose the community's current membership in a form a proof can rely on and a verifier can check. That is the interface this use case forces us to define, and it is why picking a use case that spans groups is the point rather than a complication.

07How we will know it worked

One demonstration, four tests. Nia proves the statement; the maintainer verifies it.

  • AcceptsA valid proof verifies, and a written analysis shows exactly what the verifier learned — which should be the outcome and nothing more.
  • RejectsWith Omar not a member of community C, the proof cannot be produced or does not verify. Clause 3 is doing real work.
  • UnlinkableTwo proofs from the same credentials, given to two verifiers who then compare them, cannot be linked to each other.
  • CurrentAfter Omar's membership is revoked, a proof relying on it stops verifying within the published bound.

An implementation that passes all four has demonstrated something no smaller use case could: that a trust graph can be traversed and checked without being exposed.

08Consequences

Choosing this first has costs, and they should be visible before anyone agrees to it.

It is likely to place requirements on how credentials are issued, not only on how they are presented. If so, that reaches every issuer and needs to be known early — which is why X3 asks for it to be stated up front rather than discovered late.

It gives trust registries a new obligation. Publishing something proofs can rely on is a larger change than it sounds, and revocation in particular has to be rethought so that checking whether a credential is current does not undo the privacy the proof provides.

It puts real cost on holder devices. Requirement D1 exists because a proof that only runs on a server is a proof most people cannot make for themselves.

And it commits us to a harder first milestone than a simpler demonstration would. That is the intent. A use case that could be satisfied by disclosing fewer fields would tell us nothing about whether this works.
